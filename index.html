Phrasal Verb Challenge

Understand the meaning, choose the right verb, and make it personal.

Core vocabulary

Six phrasal verbs from your lesson

come up with

think of or create an idea

“We came up with a new app idea.”
give up

stop trying

“Don't give up after one mistake.”
look up

search for information

“I'll look up the word online.”
make up

invent a story, excuse or information

“He made up an excuse.”
rely on

trust or depend on someone/something

“I rely on my team for support.”
take up

start a hobby or regular activity

“I'd like to take up photography.”
Extra useful language

Four more expressions for creative conversations

think ofwork outfind outcarry on
  • think of: produce an idea — “Can you think of a better name?”
  • work out: find an answer or solution — “Let's work out how the app will help.”
  • find out: discover information — “We need to find out what users need.”
  • carry on: continue — “Carry on! I want to hear your idea.”

Notice: some phrasal verbs have more than one meaning. Use the context to understand them.

Interactive practice

Choose the best phrasal verb

1. You don't know what a word means, so you ______ it online.
2. Our group needs to ______ a name for our app.
3. The task is difficult, but don't ______!
4. When I need advice, I ______ my best friend.
5. Don't ______ a story. Tell us what really happened.
6. I'm going to ______ tennis to get more exercise.
Make it yours

Interview your partner

Ask at least three questions. Listen carefully and ask one follow-up question.

  • What helps you COME UP WITH ideas?
  • When do you feel like GIVING UP, and what helps you CARRY ON?
  • What information do you often LOOK UP?
  • Who do you RELY ON when you need help?
  • What hobby would you like to TAKE UP?
  • How can you FIND OUT if an online story is true?
Follow-up questions: “Why?” · “Can you give me an example?” · “How did you feel?” · “What happened next?”
🔦 Phrasal verb spotlight

When you ask your partner a question, make the phrasal verb stand out. Stress the bold words and try to use the same expression in your answer.

Example: “What helps you COME UP WITH ideas?” — “I usually RELY ON my friends and LOOK UP examples online.”

04

Creative Talk · Yes, and…!

Build on ideas instead of repeating the warm-up.

You've already explored unusual uses for objects. This time, you'll practise a different creativity skill: accepting a teammate's idea and adding something new. In real conversations, collaboration often makes an idea stronger.
Round 1 · Learn the move

Don't block the idea—build on it

A: “Let's create an app that helps people spend less time online.”

B: “Yes, and it could suggest a five-minute offline activity.”

A: “Yes, and users could invite a friend to join them.”

The phrase “Yes, and…” doesn't mean you must agree with everything. It means you first show that you've listened, then add a useful possibility.

Try these:
“Yes, and we could…”
“That's interesting. We could also…”
“Building on your idea…”
“What if we added…?”
Round 2 · Team improvisation · 5 minutes

Pick a challenge

Choose one situation. Take turns adding one sentence to develop a solution. Every new sentence must build on the previous idea.

Challenge A: Your class wants to help students stop checking their phones during homework.
Challenge B: Your school wants to help students identify fake news.
Challenge C: A new student is being treated unkindly in a class group chat.
Round 3 · Share and reflect

Which idea became stronger?

Present your team's solution in 30 seconds. Then tell the class which teammate's addition helped the idea most and why.

Useful reflection:
“Our first idea was…” · “Then we added…” · “The idea became better because…” · “We relied on each other to…”

06

Design Your App · Starbursting Studio

Brainstorm, ask better questions, and build a practical solution.

Step 1 · Think alone, then share

Invent an app

Your company wants an app that helps people deal with social media problems. Your app can check information, protect privacy, manage screen time, support people experiencing cyberbullying, or solve another problem.

Step 2 · Starbursting

Ask six questions about your app

Write a question or a short answer for each point. Then discuss your answers with your group. Use “What, Who, Where, When, How, Why” to add detail.

👥 WHO?

Who will use it? Who can benefit?

❓ WHAT?

What does it do? What problem does it solve?

📍 WHERE?

Where can people use it?

🕒 WHEN?

When would people need it?

⚙️ HOW?

How does it work?

💡 WHY?

Why is it important or better?

Team discussion language:
“What if we…?” · “Could we also…?” · “I like that idea because…” · “I see your point, but…” · “Let's choose this idea because…”
Step 3 · Decide and improve

Choose your team's best idea

Compare the ideas. Choose one that is useful, easy to understand, and realistic enough to develop. Improve it using a suggestion from another team member.

Your app idea is ready. Now prepare a short pitch: explain the problem, show how your app works, and convince the class that it could help. Remember, a good pitch is clear—not complicated.

Final Pitch · Present Your Solution

Tell the class about your app and answer their questions.

Your 2-minute pitch

Use this structure

  1. The problem: “One problem with social media is…”
  2. The app: “Our app is called…”
  3. The users: “It is designed for…”
  4. How it works: “It helps by…”
  5. The benefit: “We think it is useful because…”
Opening example

“Have you ever seen a story online and wondered if it was true? Our app, FactFriend, helps users check suspicious stories before sharing them.”

Audience role

Ask one useful question

While another team presents, listen and prepare a question. Be curious and respectful.

“Who is your app designed for?”
“How does it work?”
“Why would people use it?”
“What makes it different?”
